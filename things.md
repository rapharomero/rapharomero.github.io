# Two Blind Spots in the Climate Debate: The Limits of Regulation and the Sunk Cost Trap

## Problem 1: "Just Regulate More" — Can Governments Actually Do What Climate Activists Demand?

### Formulating the Problem

A common refrain among climate advocates is that the solution lies in stronger government regulation: ban fossil fuels, implement aggressive carbon pricing, invest massively in green infrastructure. The implicit assumption is that governments *could* do this if they simply had the political will — and that failure to act is therefore a moral failing, or worse, evidence of corruption (serving "their friends" in banking and oil).

The Macron case in France is instructive. Activists portray his energy policy as a betrayal: he abolished the solidarity wealth tax (ISF), cut financial transaction taxes, and cozied up to the banking and corporate elite — all while France's carbon emissions stalled or increased. The question is: **is this primarily a case of a president protecting his wealthy friends at the expense of the planet, or is it a case of a president navigating genuine constraints where aggressive climate policy risks destabilizing the economy and triggering social unrest?**

The honest answer is: **it is both, but the constraints are far more binding than most activists acknowledge**. And understanding these constraints is essential if we want climate policy that actually works rather than policy that sounds good but triggers a backlash that sets the movement back by a decade.

### The Gilets Jaunes: When Climate Policy Met Economic Reality

The most powerful piece of evidence for the "constraints are real" argument is the gilets jaunes movement of 2018–2019. This was not an abstract thought experiment — it was a real-world natural experiment in what happens when a government implements exactly the kind of policy that climate activists demand.

In late 2018, the Macron government proposed increases to diesel and gasoline taxes as part of France's carbon pricing strategy. The policy was textbook environmental economics: increase the price of carbon-intensive fuels to reduce consumption. It was designed to meet France's Paris Agreement commitments. It was, by any reasonable standard, correct climate policy.

The result was months of violent protests that shook France to its core. The gilets jaunes protests forced Macron to suspend the tax increase and raise the minimum wage. The movement drew support from rural and suburban populations who depended on cars for commuting and could not absorb higher fuel costs without material hardship.

The critical insight from the gilets jaunes is not that French people oppose climate policy. Gilets jaunes activists were infuriated by the ways in which rising costs of transition were being individualized and directed at those least able to afford it. Their grievance was not anti-environmental — it was anti-regressive. They saw a government that had just abolished the wealth tax on the richest 5% of French citizens now asking working people to bear the cost of climate transition through higher fuel prices.

Macron scaled back France's financial transactions tax and abolished the "solidarity tax on wealth," surrendering billions of euros of revenue earmarked for social and climate policy, and cementing his reputation as "president of the rich." The sequence was lethal for the credibility of climate policy: first you cut taxes on the wealthy, then you raise fuel taxes on everyone. Whether or not Macron intended to serve his wealthy friends, the *sequence* of decisions made it impossible to argue that the costs of transition were being fairly distributed.

This is the core of the problem. If countries are transitioning to a low-carbon economy, and they don't get it right, they will see protest of the same magnitude and flavour as what was seen in France.

### But Is the Macron Critique Fair? The Real Fiscal Constraints

Now let's take seriously the possibility that Macron's choices were not purely about favoring the rich, but reflected genuine macroeconomic constraints. The data here is sobering.

For 2024, France ran a 5.8 percent deficit, almost twice the EU's limit according to the Stability and Growth Pact, which states that an EU member state's budget deficit must not exceed 3 percent of GDP. France's debt stands at approximately 112% of GDP. France is under an Excessive Debt Procedure, with the Council of the European Union adopting the recommendation to put an end to its excessive deficit by 2029.

This is not an abstract number. EU fiscal rules legally constrain how much France can spend. The new EU fiscal rules require member states above the 3% deficit limit to reduce deficit spending by a minimum of 0.5% of GDP annually. Simultaneously, closing the mitigation investment gap for France requires around 1.6% of GDP in additional annual public investment to meet the 2030 and 2050 climate targets.

These two constraints are in direct tension. The EU says: *cut your deficit by 0.5% of GDP per year*. Climate scientists say: *increase your green investment by 1.6% of GDP per year*. A government cannot do both without either raising taxes dramatically (risking social unrest and economic slowdown) or finding new revenue sources. The proposed fiscal rules prevent the fiscal stimulus that would reduce emissions, which is particularly counterproductive, as green spending has an outsized multiplier effect compared to other public investments.

In this context, the argument that Macron could simply "choose" to invest more in green infrastructure by cutting ties with bankers and oil companies becomes naive. Even a government with the best climate intentions faces real fiscal constraints. France's climate investments are insufficient, according to the Institute for Climate Economics. After rising steadily since 2020, public and private climate-related investments in 2024 dropped by 5 percent from 2023 levels. The political instability further compounds the problem: in early September 2025, Prime Minister François Bayrou's minority government fell after a no-confidence vote triggered by a controversial €44bn consolidation package.

### The Inflation Trap: Why Aggressive Climate Policy Has Real Economic Costs

Climate activists sometimes dismiss concerns about inflation and economic stability as excuses deployed by fossil fuel interests. The evidence suggests these concerns are not manufactured — they are empirically grounded.

Climate shocks have had a major impact on inflation over the last two years. The green transition has led to sharp rises in the prices of metals and minerals, while the rise in oil and gas prices has had a major impact on inflation. This phenomenon has been termed "greenflation" — inflation arising specifically from the costs of the energy transition itself.

Over-reliance on expenditure-based policies (public mitigation efforts or subsidies) threatens debt sustainability by increasing sovereign default risk and borrowing costs. Conversely, carbon-pricing policies make the transition more viable for public finances but result in higher economic costs and larger consumption losses. This is the fundamental dilemma: subsidizing the transition risks fiscal crisis; taxing carbon risks consumption crisis. There is no pain-free path.

Inflationary pressures can trigger multiple vulnerabilities: eroding purchasing power, increasing borrowing costs, dampening investment and creating political pressure to increase spending and provide social safety nets.

For a country like France, where purchasing power is already under pressure and political legitimacy is fragile, aggressive carbon pricing or massive green investment financed by debt carries a genuine risk of triggering the kind of social instability that would *set back* climate policy rather than advance it. The gilets jaunes proved this empirically.

### So What Can Governments Actually Do? A Realistic Assessment

The picture is not hopeless, but it requires honesty about what is politically and economically feasible.

**What works:**

The evidence suggests that the most promising approach is not "regulate harder" but "redistribute fairly." The most promising course, both politically and economically, is combining economic fairness with pollution control. Link decarbonization to anti-austerity policies, and the next iteration of gilets jaunes will be demonstrating in support of climate action.

Concretely, this means:

First, **carbon pricing with redistribution**. Carbon taxes are good economics but terrible politics — unless the revenue is visibly returned to citizens, especially lower-income households. The French carbon tax failed because the revenue disappeared into the general budget. The French government assigned revenues from its energy tax increase to the general budget and earmarked parts of it for a business tax credit meant to stimulate employment and competitiveness. For taxpayers, the benefits remained obscure, and credibility of the carbon tax suffered. By contrast, the Swiss carbon levy returns two-thirds of its revenue directly to residents through reduced health insurance premiums. The Canadian carbon pricing system issues quarterly "Climate Action Incentive" payments directly to households.

Second, **reforming EU fiscal rules** to exempt green investment from deficit calculations. A "green golden rule" (excluding net green investment from the fiscal indicators used to measure fiscal rule compliance) is the most promising option. Without this reform, EU member states face a structural impossibility: they cannot simultaneously reduce deficits and invest in climate transition at the required scale.

Third, **progressive taxation to finance transition**. Progressive taxation, such as a wealth tax, combined with EU early action would reduce debt by 10 percentage points in 2050 and by 31 percentage points in 2070 compared to the climate-agnostic baseline. The irony of the Macron example is that the wealth tax he abolished in 2017 could have partially financed the climate transition he committed to. Reinstating something like it, and explicitly earmarking revenues for climate investment, would simultaneously address the fairness critique and the fiscal constraint.

Fourth, and perhaps most importantly, **making the cost of inaction visible**. Delayed EU investments and insufficient adaptation results in debt levels 59 percentage points higher in 2050 and 114 percentage points higher in 2070 compared to early action. In other words, *not* investing in climate transition is more expensive than investing, on a 20–40 year horizon. The problem is that government budgets are structured around annual and five-year cycles, not 40-year horizons. Making the long-term fiscal cost of inaction explicit in budget projections would change the political calculus.

**What does not work:**

Demanding that governments simply "ban fossil fuels" or "invest massively" without addressing the fiscal constraints, distributional consequences, and political feasibility is not serious policy. It is aspirational rhetoric that, when implemented naively (as the French carbon tax was), produces backlash that entrenches opposition to climate action.

The left's blind spot here is real: the assumption that political will is the only binding constraint, and that fiscal, economic, and social constraints are either manufactured by elites or can be overcome through sheer determination. The gilets jaunes showed that this assumption is wrong. Governments that pursue climate policy without attending to distributional fairness will face revolts that undo the policy.

The right's blind spot is equally real but in the opposite direction: using the genuine constraints as a reason to do nothing, or to delay action indefinitely. The fiscal arithmetic shows clearly that delay is more expensive than action. The question is not *whether* to act but *how* to act without triggering social collapse.

### Summary: The Government Is Not a Magic Hat

The answer to "why doesn't Macron just do more?" is not that he is uniquely corrupt (though distributional choices like abolishing the ISF were genuinely damaging to climate credibility). It is that:

1. France faces legally binding EU deficit constraints that limit public spending
2. France's debt-to-GDP ratio (~114%) limits borrowing capacity
3. Carbon pricing without redistribution triggers social unrest (gilets jaunes)
4. Green investment financed by debt risks fiscal crisis and higher borrowing costs
5. Greenflation is real — the energy transition itself generates inflationary pressures
6. Political instability (five prime ministers in two years) makes long-term planning nearly impossible

None of these constraints justify inaction. All of them explain why "just regulate more" is not a sufficient answer. The realistic path involves fiscal rule reform, progressive taxation, visible redistribution, and honest communication about costs — none of which is as satisfying as "ban fossil fuels," but all of which are more likely to produce durable change.

## Problem 2: The Sunk Cost Trap — Why Attacking Fossil Fuel Workers Misses the Point

### Formulating the Problem

Climate activists routinely target fossil fuel companies and their employees as the villains of the climate story. The framing is intuitive: these companies extract and sell the substances that are destroying the climate. Their workers participate in this activity. Therefore, holding them responsible seems logical.

But this framing obscures a crucial economic reality: **fossil fuel workers and communities are trapped in a sunk cost problem that makes "just stop" economically irrational at the individual level, and socially devastating at the community level.** The problem is not that these workers don't care about the climate. It is that they face a set of economic constraints that make transition individually costly and collectively terrifying.

Understanding this trap is essential for designing climate policy that actually works — because policy that ignores the sunk cost problem will face political opposition from precisely the communities whose cooperation is needed for transition.

### The Magnitude of the Sunk Cost

The sunk costs in the fossil fuel economy operate at multiple levels:

**Individual level: Human capital and identity.** A worker who has spent 20 years developing expertise in oil drilling, coal mining, or refinery operations has invested decades in specialized skills. These skills are highly valued within the fossil fuel industry — oil and gas workers earn around 15% on average more than workers in clean energy sectors. This wage premium reflects the specificity and danger of the work, decades of union bargaining, and the concentration of fossil fuel operations in regions with few alternative employers.

Asking this worker to retrain in solar installation or wind turbine maintenance is not simply asking them to "learn a new skill." It is asking them to:

- Accept a likely pay cut during and after retraining
- Abandon skills acquired over decades that currently command premium wages
- Often relocate, because today's fossil fuel extraction workers are not co-located with current sources of green energy production
- Accept that their professional identity — the thing they are *good at* — is now socially stigmatized as planet-destroying
- Trust that the new jobs will actually materialize and last

There is no analog for mining and drilling jobs: the fossil fuel industry has them, renewables don't. The specific high-paying extraction jobs — roughnecks, drillers, coal miners — simply do not exist in the renewable sector. Construction jobs dominate the solar industry, which explains why solar energy, overall, has lower wages compared to fossil fuels.

**Community level: Economic dependency.** Fossil fuel industries are not distributed evenly across the geography. They are concentrated in specific regions — Appalachian coal country, the Permian Basin, the North Sea coast, the French Lacq gas basin. Diversified economies can more easily absorb the decline of an industry than more concentrated ones. In regions where an industry represents a large share of labor, a negative shock cannot easily be overcome, leading to long-term economic decline.

When a coal mine closes, it is not just the miners who lose their jobs. It is the truck drivers who transport coal, the restaurants that serve miners, the schools funded by coal tax revenue, the local hospitals kept alive by the community's income. It's not just the workers who are hurt by the loss of fossil fuel jobs — it's entire communities.

The parallel with the Rust Belt deindustrialization is instructive. Past examples abound, including the Rust Belt, which experienced a decline in manufacturing and steel production since the 1980s. The economic and social devastation of the Rust Belt — opioid epidemics, population decline, political radicalization — is a preview of what happens when carbon-intensive industries decline without adequate support structures.

**Corporate level: Stranded assets and path dependency.** Fossil fuel companies have billions in sunk capital: oil rigs, refineries, pipelines, coal washeries, LNG terminals. Incumbents tend to continue using coal because they are not yet required to bear high stranded assets and transition costs. Writing down these assets represents a massive loss of shareholder value. The rational corporate response is to continue extracting as long as it remains profitable — not because executives are evil, but because fiduciary duty to shareholders demands it.

### The "Just Transition" Promise: Why It Hasn't Worked

Governments have repeatedly promised "just transitions" for fossil fuel workers. The record is poor.

The Obama administration launched the POWER Plus Plan in 2015 to alleviate the economic impacts of the energy transition on coal workers and communities, but some provisions of the plan were never implemented and interagency efforts stalled under the Trump administration. Funding was chronically insufficient: In 2018, the POWER Initiative received requests for $329 million in grants, but ultimately only awarded $49 million. The gap between what was needed and what was provided was six-to-one.

Promises of a "just transition," in which millions of new energy and infrastructure jobs will more than replace any jobs lost in fossil fuel industries, give energy workers false hope, according to researchers who have studied actual transition outcomes. The reason is structural: the new jobs often do not appear in the same places, at the same wages, or with the same benefits as the old ones.

A just transition for working people needs to include financial support, in the form of cash assistance or wage replacement, to cover losses in wage income. Successful transition requires both transition assistance and high-road job growth. One without the other will leave workers, and the region, behind.

### The Data on Wage Comparisons: More Nuanced Than Either Side Admits

One of the most contested claims in the transition debate is whether green jobs pay as well as fossil fuel jobs. The evidence is mixed, and both sides cherry-pick:

**The optimistic framing:** Renewable energy jobs typically offer 8-19% higher wages than national averages and require similar skill sets to fossil fuel jobs. This is technically true — renewable energy wages are above the *national average*.

**The realistic framing:** Clean energy wage increases were on average greater than those in fossil fuels, even in major oil, gas and coal producing countries. However, this growth has been insufficient to close the prevailing wage premium enjoyed by oil and gas workers, who earn around 15% on average more than workers in clean energy sectors.

**The structural issue:** Wages for clean energy are still slightly lower than fossil fuel jobs, where wages remain high — in part due to decades of hard-fought union representation and bargaining. New green jobs are less likely to have such benefits and rights already established and workers today have less power to advocate for changes.

This is crucial. The wage premium in fossil fuels is not an accident — it is the result of decades of labor organizing. Renewable energy jobs are newer, less unionized, and more dispersed. Telling a coal miner earning $80,000 with full benefits and union protections to take a solar installation job at $55,000 without the same protections is not "transition" — it is a pay cut dressed up in green language.

**The geographic mismatch:** Even where wages are comparable, fossil fuel workers could leverage their mobility to other industries and have similar skills to green occupations. However, today's fossil fuel extraction workers are not co-located with current sources of green energy production. Green energy jobs tend to cluster in different regions than fossil fuel jobs. Asking a family to uproot from a coal community in West Virginia to take a wind turbine job in Iowa is asking far more than a career change — it is asking them to leave their community, their family networks, their home.

### The "Eat the Rich" Problem: Why Simplistic Left Critiques Fail

Your instinct is correct that the "eat the rich" framing is inadequate, and the data supports this.

The left's critique of fossil fuel companies often assumes that the primary obstacle is *greed* — that if we could just overcome the selfishness of a few billionaire executives, the transition would proceed smoothly. This misidentifies the problem in several ways:

**First, it underestimates the number of people whose livelihoods depend on fossil fuels.** Over 100,000 coal jobs have been lost since the mid-1980s in the US alone. The global figure is in the millions. These are not billionaire executives — they are working-class people with mortgages and families.

**Second, it ignores the fiscal dependencies.** Fossil fuel tax revenues fund schools, hospitals, and infrastructure in dependent regions. Within the next few decades, the more than 300 counties with active coal plants or mines will lose a significant source of tax revenue and jobs. "Eat the rich" does not answer the question of how these communities will fund their schools after the tax base disappears.

**Third, it misunderstands the inflationary mechanism.** If you rapidly shut down fossil fuel production without adequate replacement capacity, energy prices spike. Energy price spikes are regressive — they hit the poorest hardest. Inflationary pressures erode purchasing power, increase borrowing costs, and create political pressure to increase spending on social safety nets. The people chanting "eat the rich" will be the same people unable to heat their homes if the transition is botched.

**Fourth, it ignores the employment multiplier.** Investing in solar photovoltaic energy creates an average of 1.5 times as many jobs as investing the same amount of money in fossil fuels. Building efficiency retrofits create 2.8 times as many jobs as fossil fuels per dollar. This is encouraging — but these are *different* jobs in *different* places for *different* people. The aggregate job creation number does not help the individual coal miner in a region where none of these new jobs exist.

### What a Serious Transition Would Actually Require

The evidence points to a transition strategy that takes the sunk cost problem seriously rather than dismissing it:

**1. Direct income replacement, not just "retraining."** The most radical and perhaps most effective proposal comes from researchers who note that with less than 50,000 coal miners left in the U.S., the government could write every single one of them a severance check of $600,000 for $30 billion. Then they could take the money and use it to retrain, retire, start a business, or send the next generation to college. $30 billion is less than the US spends on fossil fuel subsidies in a single year. The math works. The political will does not.

**2. Place-based economic development.** Four categories of solutions are essential: workforce development and labor standards; economic development; infrastructure and environmental remediation; and public benefits. Communities cannot be told to simply "move where the green jobs are." Investment must come to where the fossil fuel communities already exist.

**3. Green jobs must match fossil fuel job quality.** We need to avoid a race to the bottom where the falling costs of green technologies are achieved by cutting wages, job security or working conditions. If the transition means replacing unionized $80,000/year fossil fuel jobs with precarious $50,000/year green jobs, workers will rationally resist. Green jobs need union protections, benefits, and wages that are competitive with what they replace.

**4. Honest timelines.** At just over 50,000 workers, U.S. coal mining hit a new low in 2019 and is expected to contract further. The decline is already happening, driven by market forces (cheap gas and renewables) more than by policy. The question is not whether these jobs will disappear but whether their disappearance will be managed or chaotic. Managed transitions require advance planning, funding commitments, and long lead times — not emergency responses after the last mine closes.

**5. Acknowledge the sunk cost honestly.** Rather than telling fossil fuel workers that they are complicit in climate destruction, acknowledge that they are trapped in a system they did not design. Their skills, communities, and identities are embedded in an industry that must decline, and the cost of that decline should be borne collectively — through progressive taxation and public investment — rather than individually by the workers and communities least able to afford it.

### Summary: The Sunk Cost Is Real, and Ignoring It Is a Political Gift to Climate Opponents

The political right has weaponized legitimate worker concerns about transition costs to block climate policy entirely. They have succeeded in part because the political left has failed to take these concerns seriously, preferring moralistic condemnation of fossil fuel companies over practical engagement with the economic realities facing workers and communities.

The data shows that:

1. Fossil fuel workers earn a genuine wage premium (~15%) over clean energy workers
2. Green jobs are not geographically co-located with fossil fuel jobs
3. Past "just transition" programs have been chronically underfunded (6:1 gap between need and funding)
4. The decline of fossil fuel industries devastates entire communities, not just individual workers
5. Direct income replacement for all US coal workers would cost ~$30 billion — less than annual fossil fuel subsidies
6. Green job quality (unionization, benefits, stability) currently lags behind fossil fuel job quality

Any serious climate policy must account for these facts. The "eat the rich" framing does not. The "just regulate more" framing does not. What is needed is a transition framework that is simultaneously ambitious on emissions and honest about costs — one that asks the wealthiest to fund the transition rather than asking the workers most affected to bear it.

## A Note on the Left-Right Framing

Your concern about sounding "right-wing" for acknowledging these constraints is itself revealing of a problem in climate discourse. The debate has been captured by a polarization in which *any* acknowledgment of economic constraints is coded as right-wing obstruction, and *any* demand for climate action is coded as left-wing idealism.

This polarization is itself an obstacle to progress. The evidence shows that:

- **The left is correct** that climate change is urgent, that fossil fuel companies have deliberately delayed action, and that the costs of transition should be borne progressively
- **The right is correct** that transition has real economic costs, that aggressive policy can trigger inflation and social instability, and that workers' material concerns are legitimate
- **Both are wrong** when they treat the other's valid concerns as bad faith

The path forward requires integrating both sets of insights: urgency *and* fiscal realism, ambition *and* distributional fairness, systemic change *and* respect for individual economic security. This is harder than either "eat the rich" or "do nothing," but it is the only approach that the evidence supports.
